What It Means

Here's the basic mechanism: when countries go to war, they order more weapons. Defense companies fulfill those orders. Their revenues go up, and their stock prices follow. This is not corruption — it is the normal functioning of the market. The companies don't start the wars. They just make what governments ask them to make.

But there's an asymmetry that matters. The cost of fighting a war is paid by the public — through taxes, through higher prices, through casualties. The profit from supplying that war is collected by a small number of private companies and their shareholders. Losses are shared. Gains are private.

The cost of war is paid by the many. The profit of war is collected by the few.
This structural asymmetry is documented throughout this report

Case Study 1 — Eastern Europe
Russia's Invasion of Ukraine
Feb 2022 – Ongoing
  • 💹 Financial winner: European defense companies. Germany's Rheinmetall rose 94% in 2025. Europe collectively added $250 billion in annual defense spending. Multi-year procurement backlogs are now booked out past 2030.
  • 🌾 Economic ripple: Ukraine and Russia supply 30% of the world's wheat. Disruptions pushed grain prices up 50% in 2022, triggering food insecurity in over 45 countries — mostly in Africa and the Middle East.
  • 💔 Human cost: Ukraine's economy shrank 29% in 2022. Infrastructure damage is estimated at $486 billion. An estimated 300,000+ combatants have died; the civilian toll is not fully counted.
The gap: The same conflict destroyed one economy and built record earnings in another. These outcomes happened simultaneously — they are not separate events.
Case Study 2 — Middle East & Red Sea
Gaza Conflict & Houthi Maritime Attacks
Oct 2023 – Ongoing
  • 🚢 Financial winner: Maritime insurance companies and missile defense manufacturers. War insurance premiums for Red Sea routes jumped 10× within weeks. RTX (Raytheon) added $8 billion to its missile defense order backlog in Q4 2023 alone.
  • 📦 Economic ripple: The Red Sea carries 12% of global trade. Ships rerouted around Africa, adding 2 weeks to delivery times. Shipping costs rose 150–300%. Egyptian Suez Canal revenues dropped 40% — a major blow to a developing country's finances.
  • 💔 Human cost: Gaza's economy contracted an estimated 83% in 2024. More than 40,000 civilians are estimated to have died as of Q1 2025. The financial beneficiaries of this conflict had no geographic proximity to the violence.
The gap: The people who profited from this conflict were in London, Connecticut, and Cape Town. The people who paid for it were in Gaza and on the Egyptian border.

Conclusion

Two ledgers. Same events. One tracks the money. The other tracks the people. Both are real. Only one gets reported in the financial news.

This is not an argument that defense investment is wrong. The ethical question — whether it is right to profit from conflict — is genuinely contested and depends on values this report cannot adjudicate.

What this report argues is simpler: the financial analysis is incomplete without the human cost data. An investment thesis that says "defense stocks outperform in wartime" is accurate. It is also missing half the story.

The half that's missing does not appear on any balance sheet. That is not an accident. It is how the accounting is designed.

"The cost of the war is borne by the many. The profit of the war is collected by the few. This has never been a secret."

This analysis examines the structural economic mechanisms that link armed conflict to corporate financial returns — and asks a question that standard financial reporting rarely poses: what is the relationship between the returns documented in the first paragraph and the deaths documented in the second?

The answer is not simple, and this report does not pretend otherwise. Defense spending serves legitimate security functions. Many of the companies examined here produce systems that have demonstrably deterred conflict escalation. The relationship between military procurement and human welfare is contested, empirically complex, and deeply dependent on context. This analysis documents the financial patterns without claiming those patterns are straightforwardly good or bad. It insists, however, that they be examined together.

How Current Spending Compares to History

To understand the current moment, context matters. Global defense spending at $2.4 trillion represents approximately 2.2% of global GDP — below the Cold War peak of roughly 4–6% of global GDP in the 1960s–80s, but significantly above the "peace dividend" lows of the mid-2000s, when spending hovered around 2% of GDP, according to SIPRI data. The post-9/11 surge (2001–2010) raised spending substantially, but was concentrated primarily in US operations. The current increase is structurally different: it is geographically distributed, with European NATO members driving the largest proportional increases since 2022.

Per capita, the disparity between the Global North and Global South is stark. The United States spends approximately $2,700 per capita annually on defense. The NATO average is roughly $950 per capita. The global average is approximately $295 per capita — with low-income countries spending a median of $40–80 per capita, many of which are simultaneously hosting active conflicts they did not initiate. (Source: SIPRI, World Bank population data, 2025)[2]

What the Market Shows

This section examines how armed conflict since 2022 has been reflected in the performance of specific equity sectors — and what industry, academic, and policy voices say about those patterns.

The financial performance of major defense contractors since February 2022 has been, by most conventional metrics, exceptional. Rheinmetall AG — Germany's largest defense manufacturer and a primary beneficiary of Europe's rearmament — saw its stock price increase by approximately 94% in the twelve months following the Ukraine invasion, and has continued to outperform. Lockheed Martin, Northrop Grumman, BAE Systems, and Raytheon Technologies (now RTX) have each generated returns substantially above the S&P 500 benchmark over the same period.

Industry representatives argue, with some legitimacy, that this performance reflects genuine demand for products that sovereign states consider essential to national security — not speculation. "Our order books reflect government decisions about how to defend their populations," a senior defense industry official said in a statement published by the Aerospace Industries Association in 2024. "This is not a business that creates the conditions it serves." The industry's position is that defense contractors fulfill contracts; they do not set foreign policy.[1]

Critics dispute the framing. William Hartung, a senior research fellow at the Quincy Institute for Responsible Statecraft and one of the leading academic analysts of the US defense industry, has written that the financial incentive structures of the sector create at minimum an interest in the persistence of threat environments. "The industry has a structural interest in a world that is seen as dangerous," Hartung wrote in a 2023 analysis. "That doesn't mean they cause wars — but it does mean they benefit from them, and that creates pressures, through lobbying and political access, that shape how threat environments are perceived and described."[3]

The Costs That Don't Appear on Balance Sheets

This section documents the human costs of active conflicts — fatalities, displacement, and daily economic burden — that are systematically absent from standard financial reporting on the defense sector.

The financial analysis in the preceding sections documents what is recorded. But a complete account of the war economy requires confronting what is not recorded — the human costs that are borne by populations entirely external to the financial transactions being analyzed.

An estimated 620,000 people have died in conflict-related violence since February 2022, according to aggregated data from ACLED and UNOCHA. Approximately 84% of casualties in contemporary armed conflict are civilians — a proportion that has risen over the past three decades as warfare has shifted from state-on-state to asymmetric and urban forms, according to the International Committee of the Red Cross.[3]

These deaths do not appear on any corporate income statement. They generate no liability in the accounts of the companies whose products may have been involved. In economic terminology, they are externalities — costs imposed on parties external to the transaction. Unlike environmental externalities, where regulatory and litigation mechanisms exist to partially internalize costs, no equivalent mechanism exists for conflict casualties. The externalization is complete and, under current international law, largely uncontested.

"There's a question that standard financial analysis never asks," notes Dr. Karina Patricio Ferreira-Duff of the International Committee of the Red Cross in a 2024 policy paper on conflict economics. "Which costs are being counted, and which aren't? When we say an investment 'performs well,' we're implicitly defining what counts as performance. The people who die in these conflicts have no column in that ledger."[9]

Three Conflicts, Three Economic Profiles

This section examines three active conflict theaters in detail — Ukraine, Gaza/Red Sea, and the Taiwan Strait — each with a distinct economic signature. For each: what sectors moved, by how much, and at what human cost.

The following case studies examine how the general mechanisms described above have manifested in three specific conflict contexts. Each has a distinct economic signature — different sectors, different magnitudes, different distributions of cost and benefit.

Case Study 1 — Eastern Europe
Russia's Invasion of Ukraine: Rearmament as Economic Event
Feb 2022 – Ongoing · Data: 2022–Q1 2026

Germany's €100 billion Sondervermögen — a constitutionally designated off-budget special fund — reversed a defense spending doctrine maintained since reunification. European defense primes entered a sustained surge-capacity period. Rheinmetall's order backlog exceeded €20 billion by Q1 2025, according to its annual report, representing multi-year guaranteed revenue.

The distributional effects are stark. Ukraine's GDP contracted by an estimated 29% in 2022, according to the World Bank. Infrastructure damage is assessed at $486 billion as of March 2024, a joint estimate by the World Bank, European Commission, and United Nations. The companies contracted to supply weapons to Ukraine reported record earnings. Both facts are true simultaneously.

"The same conflict that destroyed one economy built record earnings in another. Both happened simultaneously."
Ukraine GDP −29% · Rheinmetall backlog +€20B · 2022–2025

Agricultural commodity markets felt the impact globally. Ukraine and Russia together account for approximately 28–30% of global wheat exports. Supply disruptions were identified as a primary driver of food price inflation affecting an estimated 45 import-dependent countries, with acute humanitarian impact in North Africa and Sub-Saharan Africa, according to the World Food Programme.[8]

Case Study 2 — Middle East & Red Sea
Gaza Conflict and Houthi Maritime Interdiction: Insurance, Energy, and Supply Chain Effects
Oct 2023 – Ongoing · Data: Q4 2023–Q1 2026

The Red Sea carries approximately 12% of global trade volume, including 30% of container traffic. Houthi attacks beginning in November 2023 forced major shipping operators — Maersk, MSC, CMA CGM — to reroute vessels around the Cape of Good Hope, adding 10–14 days to Asia-Europe transit times.

Case Study 3 — Indo-Pacific
Taiwan Strait: Pre-Conflict Capital Positioning and the Semiconductor Risk Premium
Ongoing strategic tension · AUKUS / CHIPS Act active · Scenario-based analysis

The Taiwan Strait represents the highest-magnitude latent risk scenario in current global capital markets. Unlike Ukraine or Gaza, no active armed conflict has commenced — yet defense procurement, semiconductor supply chain restructuring, and naval buildup are already generating substantial capital flows. This constitutes a pre-conflict asset allocation event with no clear precedent in post-Cold War market history.

Taiwan's centrality in the global semiconductor supply chain is the critical variable. TSMC alone produces approximately 90% of the world's most advanced chips. Even a non-kinetic coercive scenario — blockade, quarantine, sustained military pressure — would generate supply shocks across automotive, consumer electronics, defense, and AI infrastructure sectors globally. Bloomberg Economics has modeled the first-year global GDP impact of a Taiwan blockade at over $5 trillion.

AUKUS submarine procurement, US INDOPACOM force posture investment, and the CHIPS Act-funded semiconductor onshoring drive represent capital flows already materially influenced by Taiwan Strait risk premium — without a single shot having been fired. The conflict is, in capital terms, already partially priced in.[2]

The Arguments Against This Framing

Any account of defense sector economics that claims credibility must engage seriously with the strongest counterarguments — not as a formality, but as genuine challenges to the analytical framework presented above. There are at least four such challenges that deserve sustained attention.

1. The Deterrence Argument

The most substantive challenge to conflict-as-investment-signal framing is the deterrence argument: that military spending, when correctly calibrated, prevents wars rather than enabling them. NATO's Article 5 collective defense commitment, supported by sustained US and European military investment, is widely credited by security scholars with preventing direct military conflict between major powers in Europe for eight decades. The financial returns documented in this report may, on this view, be the cost of a relative peace that would not otherwise exist.

"The question isn't whether defense spending is profitable," argues Professor Theo Farrell of University College Dublin, a leading scholar of military effectiveness. "The question is what security outcome it purchases. Deterrence that works doesn't produce headlines — it produces the absence of events. That makes it very hard to measure, and very easy to dismiss."[10] The counterargument to this position is that deterrence theory, while theoretically sound, has historically been used to justify procurement levels that exceeded any plausible deterrence requirement — a critique documented extensively in the academic literature on military-industrial lobbying.

2. The ESG Performance Challenge

The approximately $3.8 trillion in assets managed under defense exclusion mandates has, since 2022, experienced measurable performance costs relative to unconstrained benchmarks. This creates a genuine dilemma for ESG-oriented institutions: a values-aligned portfolio that has underperformed its benchmark by several percentage points represents real capital not available for other social purposes. Some institutional investors have quietly relaxed defense exclusions; others have held the line at increasing cost to beneficiaries.[5]

The ESG debate in the defense sector is further complicated by the dual-use problem: the technologies developed by major defense primes — satellite communications, cybersecurity, supply chain software — have extensive civilian applications. The boundary between "defense contractor" and "technology company" has become commercially and ethically blurred in ways that categorical exclusion screens struggle to address.

3. Bubble Risk and the Valuation Question

The financial case for defense sector investment is not without its own skeptics. Rheinmetall's forward P/E ratio expanded from approximately 12x in 2021 to 28x by late 2025 — a multiple that effectively prices in sustained conflict-level procurement indefinitely. A negotiated settlement in Ukraine, or a significant reduction in European threat perception, would likely trigger rapid and significant multiple compression. Investors entering the sector at current valuations are, in a meaningful sense, betting on the persistence of instability.

Several sell-side analysts have begun flagging this risk in their coverage. "We maintain our constructive view on the sector, but acknowledge that current multiples leave limited margin for disappointment," wrote analysts at a major European investment bank in a sector note published in December 2025. "Any diplomatic progress on the Ukraine front would likely result in a sharp de-rating."

4. The "Merchants of Death" Historical Parallel

The contemporary debate about defense investment has a direct historical antecedent. The Senate Munitions Investigating Committee, chaired by Senator Gerald Nye, held hearings between 1934 and 1936 examining the role of arms manufacturers in the United States' entry into World War I. The committee's findings — controversial then and still debated by historians — alleged that financial interests in Allied victory had influenced the US government's path toward war. The term "merchants of death" entered the political lexicon.

The contemporary situation differs in important respects: modern defense contractors operate under considerably more stringent regulatory oversight, the wars in question were not initiated by Western states, and the causal relationship between arms sales and conflict escalation is more contested empirically. But the underlying structure of the question — who profits, who pays, and how those two groups relate to the decisions that produced the conflict — remains recognizably similar.[11]

What This Analysis Cannot Tell You

This report has documented, with as much precision as publicly available data allows, the financial mechanisms linking armed conflict to corporate returns. It has also attempted to document, with equal precision, the human costs that are systematically excluded from that financial picture. What it cannot do is tell you what to conclude.

The normative questions raised by this analysis — whether it is ethical to profit from defense sector investment, whether defense procurement serves deterrence or enables escalation, whether ESG exclusion mandates reflect genuine values or impose costs on beneficiaries without achieving security outcomes — are genuinely contested. Reasonable people, examining the same data presented here, reach different conclusions based on different underlying value frameworks.

What this report insists on is that these questions be asked together. The financial analysis and the humanitarian analysis are not two separate stories. They are the same story, seen from different ledgers. A complete account of the war economy requires both columns: the returns, and the costs that never appear.

Technical Glossary

Offset Agreement›

A condition imposed by a purchasing government requiring the defense contractor to invest a percentage of the contract value in the buyer's domestic economy — through local manufacturing, technology transfer, or subcontracting. Standard in international defense procurement above $50M. Offsets complicate headline contract valuations and are a significant negotiating variable in major arms deals.

Multi-Year Procurement (MYP)›

A US defense contracting mechanism allowing DoD to commit to purchasing a specified quantity of a weapon system over multiple years (typically 3–5) in exchange for a lower per-unit price. MYP contracts provide defense contractors with extended revenue visibility. They are a key driver of the sector's countercyclical revenue profile and its resistance to standard macroeconomic downturns.

Negative Externality›

An economic term for a cost imposed on a party not involved in the originating transaction. Civilian casualties in armed conflict are negative externalities of defense procurement — costs borne by affected populations but not reflected in the price or liability structure of any procurement contract. Unlike environmental externalities, no regulatory or litigation mechanism currently exists to internalize these costs at source.

War Risk Premium›

An additional surcharge on maritime or aviation insurance for assets operating in or transiting conflict zones. Highly sensitive to geopolitical events — premiums can increase tenfold within days of a conflict escalation, as observed in the Red Sea from late 2023. Collected by insurers and reinsurers as revenue; represents a direct financial mechanism linking conflict intensity to insurance sector returns.

ESG Exclusion Screen›

An investment mandate provision prohibiting holdings in sectors on environmental, social, or governance grounds. Defense sector exclusions are among the most common ESG screens in European institutional investment. Approximately $3.8T in AUM is currently managed under mandates with explicit defense restrictions (Morningstar, Q4 2024). The scope varies: some exclude all defense contractors; others exclude only manufacturers of controversial weapons (cluster munitions, landmines, nuclear warheads).

Sondervermögen›

German: "special fund." A constitutionally permissible off-budget debt vehicle allowing the German government to fund expenditure outside its constitutional debt brake (Schuldenbremse). The €100B Sondervermögen announced in February 2022 was earmarked exclusively for Bundeswehr modernization — the largest single defense investment in post-war German history. It signaled a structural change in German defense policy with significant implications for the European defense industrial base.

Private Military Company (PMC)›

A private sector entity providing military or security services under contract to state or non-state clients, including combat operations, training, intelligence, and site protection. PMCs operate in a fragmented international legal framework with variable accountability under domestic law and inconsistent coverage under international humanitarian law. The sector has recorded the highest conflict-correlated financial return of any category analyzed in this report.

Pigouvian Tax / Corrective Mechanism›

A tax or regulatory mechanism designed to make the originator of a negative externality bear its cost — named after British economist Arthur Pigou. Carbon taxes are the best-known modern example. No equivalent mechanism currently exists for conflict-related civilian harm: no company pays a "harm tax" on weapons that kill civilians. The absence of such a mechanism is a structural feature of the current international legal and economic order, not an oversight.

Historical Defense Spending Context

Cold War peak (1960s–80s): Global defense spending reached approximately 4–6% of global GDP at its height. Current level (~2.2%) remains well below this, though the trend is upward.

Post-9/11 surge (2001–2010): US-led operations drove increases concentrated in a single country. The current increase is structurally different — distributed across 22+ NATO states with legislated floors.

Merchants of Death precedent: The US Senate Nye Committee (1934–36) examined whether arms industry financial interests influenced US entry into WWI. The contemporary regulatory environment differs significantly, but the structural question it posed remains live.
